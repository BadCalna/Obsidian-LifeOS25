> [!important] ## Topic
> SMART Methodology
## S.M.A.R.T
- S - Specific: ***Is the question specific? Does it address the problem? Does it have context? Will it uncover a lot of the information you need?***
	- simple, significant, and focused on a **single topic** or a few closely related ideas.
> [!example]  Example
>  ❌"Are kids getting enough exercise everyday?"
>  ✅"What percentage of kids achieve recommended 60 minutes of physical activity at least five days a week?"
- M - Measurable: ***Will the question give you answers that you can measure?***
	- Can be quantified and assessed
> [!example]  Example
>  ❌"Why did our recent video go viral?"
>  ✅"How many times was our video shared on social channels the first week it was posted?"
- A - Action-oriented: ***Will the answers provide information that helps you devise some type of plan?***
	- Encourage change
	  How to understand this? **Problem solving finds out how to turn the current situation to the ideal one, Action-oriented question revealing the path to the turning.**
> [!example]  Example
>  ❌"How can we get customer to recycle our product packaging?" -- **This not aim for the answer that clearing next action**.
>  ✅"What design feature will make our packaging easier to recycle?"
- R - Relevant: ***Is the question about the particular problem you are trying to solve?***
	- Have significance to the problem you are trying to solve
> [!example]  Example
>  ❌"Why does it matter that Pine Barrens tree frogs started disappearing?”
>  ✅“What environmental factors changed in Durham,North Carolina, between 1983 and 2004 that could cause Pine Barrens tree frogs to disappear from the Sandhills Regions?
- T - Time-bound: ***Are the answers relevant to the specific time being studied?***

>[!example] ## Ineffective&Bad question example
- ***Leading questions***: lead you to a certain answer
	- **You can't make interviewee share their own points of view.**
	- eg. These sandwich are the best ever, aren't they? 
- ***Closed-ended questions***: Can simply answered by 'Yes' or 'No'
	- **This kind of  question cannot get rarely insights.
- ***Too vague and lack context***: Just like those MBTI question

> [!EXAMPLE] ## Examples of SMART questions —— Breaking one questions into several SMART questions
> ⭕️**What features do people look for when buying a new car?**
> Here comes the SMART thoughts:
> 🅢 - The question is focus on a particular feature or just casual feature?
> 🅜 - Is there a rating system on those features?
> 🅐 - Does the question influence creation of different or new feature packages?
> 🅡 - Is this question identify a feature makes or breaks a potential car purchase?
> 🅣 - Do we focus on the last three years' data or longer?
> ### Tips: Questions should be **open-ended**. In that way you can get the data you really need. Here comes the possible questions:
> - On a scale of 1-10 (with 10 being the most important) how important is your car having four-wheel drive? Explain.
>   ***Measurable&Specific***
> - What are the top five features you would like to see in a car package?
>   ***Action-oriented***
> - What features, if included with four-wheel drive, would make you more inclined to buy the car?
>   ***Relevant&Specific***
> - How does a car having four-wheel drive contribute to its value, in your opinion?
>   ***Specific&Relevant***



> [!Tips] ## Key takeaways
- Ensure Fairness when asking(ref. [[10.4.1 Data Fairness]])
	- don't create or reinforce bias
	- make the question meaningful for every one take the survey

> [!note] ## Self-Reflection
- When preparing a set of questions, there are several things need to be focused on:
	- **Prioritize your questions**: Important questions first
	- **Make your time count**: Don't miss out! Stay on the subject!
	- **Clarify your understanding**: Clarify what you get from time to time
	- If a question is worth asking, then the answer is worth recording. Commit yourself to taking great notes during your conversation.

> [!Example] ## Some instance
> For instance, if you have a conversation with someone who works in retail, you might lead with questions like: 
- Specific: Do you currently use data to drive decisions in your business? If so, what kind(s) of data do you collect, and how do you use it?
- Measurable: Do you know what percentage of sales is from your top-selling products?
- Action-oriented: Are there business decisions or changes that you would make if you had the right information? For example, if you had information about how umbrella sales change with the weather, how would you use it?
- Relevant: How often do you review data from your business?
- Time-bound: Can you describe how data helped you make good decisions for your store(s) this past year?
If you are having a conversation with a **teacher**, you might ask different questions, such as: 
- Specific: What kind of data do you use to build your lessons?
- Measurable: How well do student benchmark test scores correlate with their grades?
- Action-oriented: Do you share your data with other teachers to improve lessons?
- Relevant: Have you shared grading data with an entire class? If so, do students seem to be more or less motivated, or about the same?
- Time-bound: In the last five years, how many times did you review data from previous academic years?
If you are having a conversation with **a small business owner** of an ice cream shop, you could ask:
- Specific: What data do you use to help with purchasing and inventory?
- Measurable: Can you order (rank) these factors from most to least influential on sales: price, flavor, and time of year (season)?
- Action-oriented: Is there a single factor you need more data on so you can potentially increase sales?
- Relevant: How do you advertise to or communicate with customers?
- Time-bound: What does your year-over-year sales growth look like for the last three years?
